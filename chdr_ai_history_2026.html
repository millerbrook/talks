printmigrationnetwork.org

[1:10–2:15]

PRINT, People, Religion, Information Networks and Travel, gathers Quaker, Anabaptist and Pietist correspondence from five repositories in the US, England, Germany and the Netherlands, in English, Dutch and German. It is funded by the NHPRC through 2026, and volunteers on Zooniverse help transcribe the letters.

Rosalind Beiler's thesis: these minority religious networks carried far more than migration. They carried business, family news and politics.

Researchers built the structure: names and places standardized as linked open data, with variant spellings kept; 169 nested keywords, plus modern "altkeywords" such as money for specie; and twelve kinds of mention, including trip, encounter, birth and marriage.

Machine learning is being piloted on top of that structure. Named-entity recognition proposes people and places. Topic modeling groups passages by theme. Language models are being tested for reading across the three languages. These are pilots.

The machine's output is a suggestion that lands in a structure researchers designed, so they can check it.

the network as context, not conclusion

PRINT portal, network view · 365 nodes, 517 edges · July 2025 build

[2:15–3:30]

You choose what counts as a connection. Nodes can be people, organizations, religions or documents. Ties can be sender, receiver, author, waypoint, or mentioned. Writing to someone and being mentioned by someone are different ties, and the portal keeps them apart. A time slider shows ties forming and fading across the century.

The familiar objection: network graphs look like an objective, top-down truth about a community, and they say little about the ideas that drive communication. PRINT turns this around. The network is the context you read a letter, or a bundle of letters, against.

Example: an account of slavery in the colonies may seem forceful because it occurs repeatedly. If the letters carrying it cluster in an isolated community, cut off from most of the network, its influence looks different.

Wistar to Hölzer, 20 November 1736 · Wistar Family Papers, Historical Society of Pennsylvania · illustrative walk-through

[3:30–4:20]

Dr. Beiler's Wistar story: in 1736 Caspar Wistar, a Philadelphia Quaker merchant, orders custom rifles from Georg Friedrich Hölzer in the Neckar Valley, to be sent with migrants. He tells Hölzer to check with Jacob Schnäbeli, an Anabaptist elder in Mannheim, who will know who is migrating.

Named-entity recognition proposes the names and places. A researcher accepts, merges or rejects each one, assigns a mention type, and flags anything uncertain. PRINT's mention records have uncertainty fields for person, place, date and keyword.

Schnäbeli never writes a letter in this exchange, yet he is a hub of migration knowledge. Mentions put people like him into the network, people the sender and receiver metadata alone would miss.

NER is still a pilot, so this is how the workflow is designed to run.

route, don't pick

  • each collection goes to the model that reads it best
  • a second model re-reads the image and the draft
  • people correct what is flagged; corrections become training data
  • first pilot: a PRINT collection

[4:20–5:30]

This is a proposed test system, not a production tool. This page switches from Latin to English mid-page, is written in secretary hand, and abbreviates words like p-bar-dict. No single model handles all of that best.

Frontier vision models now lead on English. Fine-tuned specialist models still win on many non-English collections. Some models quietly modernize spelling, which historians can't accept. So the system sends each collection to the cheapest model that meets the quality bar: a fine-tuned open model, a frontier vision model, or a layout-aware OCR engine.

A second model then re-reads the image together with the draft and marks [?] or [illegible]. In one study, an LLM correcting conventional HTR output cut the character error rate from 5.7% to 1.8% (Humphries et al., 2025). People correct only the flagged spans, and each verified correction becomes training and test data.

The first pilot will be a PRINT collection. Multilingual early-modern letters are exactly the case where one model isn't enough.

one word, three dictionaries

[5:30–6:30]

Both Webster editions are encoded in TEI-XML and linked to their page images, so every sense is a structured record you can cite back to the page. A vector database then scores how similar passages are, within and across dictionaries. That supports the project's questions: what changed between 1806 and 1828, how Webster's entries compare to Johnson's, his approach to etymology and definition, and his use of American vocabulary.

Cabinet: five of Johnson's senses carry into 1828, mostly with the same authorities: Bacon, Dryden, Swift, Taylor, Spenser. Webster reorders them. Johnson's second sense, "a hut or small house," drops to last and is marked obsolete. The etymology grows from Johnson's "cabinet, French" to French, Italian and Spanish forms, with a cross-reference to cabin.

I matched these senses by hand from the page images. Similarity scoring does the same matching across the whole dictionary.

what Webster added

"the executive counsel of a state"

"The select or secret council of a prince or executive government; so called from the apartment in which it was originally held."

— Webster, 1828

[6:30–7:10]

The one sense in 1828 with no match in Johnson is the political one. Johnson's nearest is "a private room in which consultations are held." Webster already has "the executive counsel of a state" in 1806. By 1828 it is a full sense, and he explains the name by the room where the council met.

That is the kind of difference the similarity scores surface at scale: Webster bringing American political usage into the dictionary, one sense at a time.

Webster for K–12 readers

"The select or secret council of a prince or executive government; so called from the apartment in which it was originally held."

— Webster, 1828

A group of top advisors to the President or leader of a country who help make important decisions. They're called a "cabinet" because they used to meet in a small private room.

— modern English, AI-drafted, editor-reviewed

[7:10–8:00]

Read both aloud. Students stumble on "select or secret council of a prince." The modern version makes it clear these are the President's top advisors.

Three design choices. The reader chooses whether to show the translation. It sits beside the original rather than replacing it. It is AI-drafted and reviewed by an editor before it goes live. The point is to get students into Webster's own words, not around them.

long-lived projects, long-lived code

  • coding agents are good at finding bugs
  • older sites: audit the code for vulnerabilities, then patch
  • new sites: test against current coding practices before launch
  • developers review every proposed fix

[8:00–9:00]

One more use of AI, and this one is behind the scenes. Digital history projects live a long time. Some of the active projects we maintain have codebases going back to 2011, when PHP was the dominant language for websites like ours. That code was written to the practices of its day, and the people who wrote it have often moved on.

It turns out coding agents are very good at finding bugs. So we will use them in two ways. On older sites, an agent reads through the code and flags vulnerabilities and outdated practices so we can patch them. On new sites, it tests the code against current best practices before launch.

The same rule applies here as everywhere else in this talk: the agent finds and proposes, and a developer reviews every fix before it goes live.

Why this matters to historians: a digital edition is only useful as long as it stays online and trustworthy. Security is part of preservation.
